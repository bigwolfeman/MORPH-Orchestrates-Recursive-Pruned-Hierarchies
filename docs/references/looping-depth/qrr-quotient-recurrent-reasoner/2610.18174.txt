                                         1        I NTRODUCTION

                                         Looped reasoners trade architectural depth for recurrent computation (Dehghani et al., 2019; Saunshi
                                         et al., 2025; Fan et al., 2025). A shared neural block is applied repeatedly to refine an internal state,
                                         allowing additional computation without increasing parameter count (Bansal et al., 2022; Geiping
                                         et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025). Recent works make the dynamical
                                         interpretation explicit, viewing reasoning as the evolution of a latent state converging toward solution-
                                         aligned equilibria (Blayney et al., 2026; Kim, 2026). Fixed-Point Reasoning Model (FPRM) uses
                                         convergence of the recurrent state to a fixed point as an intrinsic stopping criterion, Attractor Models
                                         define the prediction itself as the equilibrium of a learned refinement process, and Equilibrium
                                         Reasoners (EqR) explicitly learns task-conditioned attractors whose stable fixed points correspond to
                                         valid solutions, using multiple initializations to explore their attraction basins (Movahedi et al., 2026;
                                         Fein-Ashley & Rashidinejad, 2026; Huang et al., 2026). In this view, reasoning is not just a sequence
                                         of refinements but a recurrent trajectory through the learned dynamical landscape.
                                         However, viewing recurrent computation as convergence to a stable attractor does not capture the
                                         full range of dynamics it can exhibit. Trajectories can instead exhibit orbits, drifts, cyclic limiting
                                         behavior, and other structured forms of non-stationarity, rather than simply relaxing toward a single
                                         fixed point (Geiping et al., 2025; Pappone et al., 2025; Blayney et al., 2026; Kim, 2026; Yang et al.,
                                         2026). These studies reveal a wide range of latent geometries in recurrent models, yet how these
                                         different forms of oscillatory motion affect the underlying latent computation remains unexplored.
                                         The key distinction is therefore not the geometry of the motion itself, but whether and how it
                                         influences the recurrent computation. Around a solution root z ⋆ , where the recurrence admits a fixed
                                         linearization with the Jacobian J = Df (z ⋆ ), we establish a local coupling mechanism by separating
                                         an oscillatory spectral coordinate from the complementary latent dynamics and quantifying how the
                                         former drives the latter. When the oscillatory mode decays more slowly, this coupling transfers its rate

2      BACKGROUND
2.1      L OOPED REASONING MODELS
Looped reasoning models increase effective computational depth by repeatedly applying shared
neural blocks to a latent state, allowing additional test-time computation without increasing parameter
count. Recent approaches can be broadly grouped by how they organize this recurrent computa-
tion. General recurrent-depth models iterate a shared Transformer block and scale reasoning by
increasing the number of latent recurrences at inference (Geiping et al., 2025). Puzzle-oriented
recursive reasoners instead structure the recurrence explicitly. HRM couples high- and low-level
recurrent modules operating at different frequencies (Wang et al., 2025), whereas Tiny Reasoning
Model (TRM) simplifies this hierarchy to a single small network that repeatedly refines a latent
reasoning state and the current answer (Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025). A complementary line organizes
recurrent computation around equilibria or attractors: FPRM uses convergence to a fixed point as
an adaptive halting criterion (Movahedi et al., 2026), EqR learns task-conditioned attractors whose
stable equilibria correspond to solutions (Huang et al., 2026), and Attractor Models explicitly solve a
learned fixed-point refinement with implicit differentiation (Fein-Ashley & Rashidinejad, 2026).
2.2      G AUGE SYMMETRY AND QUOTIENT DYNAMICS
Gauge symmetry formalizes a local representational redundancy where changing the local reference
frame changes the coordinates used to represent a state, but not the underlying object itself (Cohen
et al., 2019a; Bronstein et al., 2021; Gerken et al., 2023). If a map T is equivariant under the action
of a group G, gauge-related states z ∼ g · z evolve consistently and can be identified as a single
equivalence class [z] in the quotient space Z/G (Kondor & Trivedi, 2018; Cohen et al., 2019b). The
quotient collapses symmetry-related representations into a single state, so motion along the symmetry

orbit is ignored while all remaining state changes are preserved. This distinction also changes the
notion of convergence. An equivariant system may continue moving along a symmetry orbit without
changing its quotient state. Such motion is captured by a relative equilibrium, T (z ⋆ ) = g ⋆ · z ⋆ ,
which is non-stationary in the original coordinates but becomes a fixed point after quotienting,
[T (z ⋆ )] = [z ⋆ ] (Krupa, 1990; Marsden & Ratiu, 1999). In neural architectures, gauge equivariance
provides a principled way to implement this invariance to local reference frames (Weiler et al., 2021;
Wiersma et al., 2020). Gauge-equivariant convolutions extend conventional equivariance from global
transformations to local gauge choices on manifolds (Cohen et al., 2019a), and Gauge Equivariant
Mesh CNNs combine anisotropic convolution with parallel transport to communicate features in
different local frames (Haan et al., 2021). The same principle has also been extended to attention,
where the Gauge Equivariant Transformer constructs gauge-equivariant self-attention by comparing
features across local coordinate systems after transporting them to a common frame (He et al., 2021).
2.3   O SCILLATORY AND NON - STATIONARY DYNAMICS IN RECURRENT REASONING
Recent depth-recurrent language models exhibit structured latent trajectories that are substantially
richer than monotone convergence to a fixed point, including multi-dimensional orbits and approx-
imately one-dimensional drifts, particularly on tokens requiring extended computation (Geiping
et al., 2025; Pappone et al., 2025; Kim, 2026). Other mechanistic studies find cyclic fixed-point
trajectories, where recurrent layers repeatedly trace a stable cycle in latent space, as well as persistent
non-stationarity even after successive state differences become small (Huang et al., 2026; Blayney
et al., 2026). Oscillatory behavior also appears in recurrent reasoners based on solution equilibria.
FPRM, for example, reports oscillation around solution fixed points and introduces damping to
stabilize these trajectories (Movahedi et al., 2026). More broadly, looped Transformers with the same
architecture, objective, and task performance can settle into different dynamical phases, including
bounded non-stationary motion generated by complex-conjugate Jacobian modes (Kim, 2026).
What remains unclear is the dynamical role of this motion. An observed orbit or alternating trajectory
may support recurrent computation, or instead reflect a slowly decaying mode that unnecessarily
prolongs latent settlement after the remaining dynamics already converged.

3     R ATE -L IMITING S LOW O SCILLATORY M ODES IN R ECURRENT R EASONERS
Motivated by oscillatory structures observed in recurrent reasoners, we study whether an identified
oscillatory mode can become rate-limiting for latent convergence. Around solution roots, the nonlinear
recurrence admits a stationary first-order linearization, allowing us to isolate an oscillatory spectral
component from its complementary latent dynamics.
We use a Sylvester decomposition as an analytic attribution tool to separate the latent state dynamics
into its intrinsic evolution and the response induced by coupling with the identified spectral coordinate.
This provides a direct way to quantify how the identified coordinate influences the remaining latent
dynamics and their convergence behavior. The decomposition itself is general to any selected mode,
particular oscillatory structure enters through local Jacobian block identified from the trained model.

4     Q UOTIENT DYNAMICS CONVERGENCE
Our analysis in Section 3 shows that slowly decaying oscillatory modes can limit convergence speed
in a locally linearized regime near a solution root. This does not, however, justify suppressing
oscillatory motion throughout the entire reasoning trajectory. Rotational or alternating dynamics
may still participate in the reasoning computation, and removing them indiscriminately can disrupt
reasoning. Indeed, persistently suppressing an alternating trajectory component in FPRM before
output stabilization degrades task performance (see Appendix A.4.4). Rather than attempting to
directly correct the oscillatory bottlenecks identified above, QRR takes a complementary architectural
approach by introducing an exact token-local gauge symmetry.
Importantly, QRR does not assume any correspondence between these gauge directions and the
slow modes identified in other reasoners. QRR learns its own recurrent trajectory. The symmetry
instead introduces token-local rotational degrees of freedom whose motion is separated from the
task-relevant quotient dynamics. How this symmetry changes the learned dynamics is therefore an
empirical question: QRR still retains oscillatory modes in quotient space, but does not develop the
isolated dominant slow mode observed in FPRM or HRM.

4.1   T OKEN - LOCAL GAUGE SYMMETRY
Let the recurrent state consist of S token states h = (h1 , . . . , hS ), with hs ∈ Cd , together with
momentum states ms ∈ Cd . In our implementation, each real-valued token state ys ∈ R2d is
reinterpreted as hs ∈ Cd through the fixed pairing hs,c = ys,2c−1 + i ys,2c , c = 1, . . . , d. The input
representation x is gauge-neutral. We introduce the group G = U (1)S , with one phase ϕs for each
token. For gϕ = (eiϕ1 , . . . , eiϕS ) ∈ G, the group action is

                    gϕ · (h, m) = (eiϕs hs )Ss=1 , (eiϕs ms )Ss=1 ,
                                                                 
                                                                         gϕ · x = x.                 (16)

Thus, all complex channels within a token share a common phase, while different tokens may
rotate independently. Equivalently, in real coordinates, let J denote the block-diagonal infinitesimal
generator of the phase rotation, so that the gauge tangent associated with token s is generated by J ys
(or, equivalently, by ihs in complex coordinates). We require the recurrent map T to be G-equivariant
while the prediction readout R is G-invariant,
               T (g · z; x) = g · T (z; x),     R(g · z; x) = R(z; x),         z = (h, m).             (17)
Hence, states that differ only by token-local phase are identified as the same task-relevant state, and
motion along the corresponding gauge directions is invisible to the prediction readout.
4.2   Q UOTIENT DYNAMICS
The symmetry identifies all states on the same group orbit, [z] = G · z = {g · z : g ∈ G}, yielding the
quotient state space Q = Z/G. Because the recurrent map is G-equivariant, it induces a well-defined
map on the quotient,
                                        T ([z]; x) = [T (z; x)].                                   (18)
The quotient removes only symmetry-generated degrees of freedom, while preserving all remaining
latent variation. Accordingly, a state may satisfy T (z ⋆ ; x) = g ⋆ · z ⋆ for some g ⋆ ∈ G, making z ⋆
a relative equilibrium where the unreduced trajectory may continue along the group orbit, while
T ([z ⋆ ]; x) = [z ⋆ ] is an ordinary fixed point in the quotient. Moreover, exact equivariance ensures that
a perturbation along the gauge orbit remains gauge-tangent after the recurrent update, and therefore
produces no change in the quotient state.

5     E XPERIMENTS
Datasets and baselines We evaluate on four reasoning benchmarks covering constraint satisfaction,
path finding, and abstract transformation. Sudoku-Extreme consists of challenging partially filled
9 × 9 Sudoku puzzles with unique solutions; following the standard HRM protocol, we train on 1,000
base puzzles with 1,000× augmentation and evaluate using exact sequence accuracy on the full test
set (Wang et al., 2025). Maze-Hard is a 30 × 30 shortest-path benchmark with 1,000 training and
1,000 test mazes. We follow the FPRM setup with no augmentation (Movahedi et al., 2026). For
Mini-ARC, a compact 5 × 5 ARC variant for abstract rule induction (Kim et al., 2022; Huang et al.,
2026), we use the released tasks with 100 data augmentations per task. We additionally evaluate
on Maze-Unique, following the EqR construction. Unlike Maze-Hard, where multiple optimal
paths may correspond to a single supervised target, Maze-Unique uses perfect tree-structured mazes
with a unique start–goal solution (Kim et al., 2022). This provides a cleaner convergence analysis,
avoiding the ambiguous solution geometry that can hinder formation of a single stable basin (Huang
et al., 2026). We compare against HRM, TRM, EqR, and FPRM, spanning hierarchical recurrence,
recursive refinement, learned attractors, and fixed-point adaptive halting.

6    C ONCLUSION
We studied how non-stationary latent dynamics constrain convergence in recurrent reasoners. Our
analysis shows that a slowly decaying oscillatory mode can transmit its rate to coupled latent dynamics
and become the bottleneck to settlement, while direct suppression of such motion can also disrupt
the reasoning computation. This motivates QRR, which does not eliminate oscillations based on
their dynamical appearance, but instead introduces an exact token-local gauge symmetry under
which symmetry-generated motion leaves the quotient state unchanged by construction. Exact gauge
equivariance prevents this motion from forcing the quotient dynamics, allowing convergence and
halting to be defined modulo the symmetry. Across various reasoning tasks, QRR achieves strong
reasoning performance while reaching solution roots much earlier and supporting reliable adaptive
halting. These results suggest that convergence in recurrent reasoning is better understood in terms of
task-relevant quotient space than raw latent space.

A.3     QRR A RCHITECTURE

QRR follows the recurrent-block organization of FPRM, but replaces the recurrent trunk with the
gauge-equivariant construction described in Section 4.3 and augments recurrence with momentum.

Input and recurrent state. Let x denote the embedded task input, which is kept fixed and re-
injected at every recurrent step. The recurrent state consists of a latent state yt and a momentum
state mt , both transforming covariantly under the token-local G = U (1)S action. In real coordinates,
yt , mt ∈ RS×2d , with the fixed identification R2d ≃ Cd used throughout the gauge-equivariant
trunk. For each input, we initialize m0 = 0 and draw y0 independently at random. Specifically, each
four-dimensional real cell of y0 is sampled coordinate-wise from a truncated normal distribution and
normalized to unit norm.

   A.4.4                                FPRM FLIP MODE SUPPRESSION
  The local analysis in Section 3 shows that the slowly decaying flip mode can limit late-stage
  convergence in FPRM. This does not imply, however, that the same mode is dispensable throughout
  reasoning. We therefore test whether persistently suppressing the alternating component before the
  output has stabilized interferes with the computation itself.
  For each trajectory, we estimate a one-dimensional alternating direction U from the preceding
  recurrent updates and, from an intervention time t0 onward, modify every subsequent update dt as

 We use α = 12 as the suppression intervention and compare against the unmodified rollout. Early
 interventions start before the native output-stabilization (decoded output stabilizes and remains equal
 for the remainder of the rollout), whereas late interventions start after stabilization. To distinguish
 suppression of the oscillatory mode from a generic reduction in update magnitude, we additionally
 suppress a non-oscillatory control direction while matching the removed energy. All comparisons
 are paired against the corresponding original rollout, and confidence intervals are obtained by
 bootstrapping over boards.
  Early suppression decreases final exact accuracy by 5.9 percentage points, and delays output sta-
  bilization. In contrast, removing a comparable amount of update energy along a non-oscillatory
  direction produces no statistically distinguishable degradation, and suppressing the flip mode only
  after the output has stabilized has no measurable effect. These results indicate that the oscillatory
  motion of the same alternating form can still participate in the earlier reasoning computation. This
  motivates QRR to avoid suppressing oscillations based on their dynamical appearance alone, and
  instead quotient out only motion whose representational redundancy is guaranteed by symmetry.

A.5.1                                 R EASONING TASKS

We evaluate on four reasoning benchmarks. Sudoku-Extreme consists of partially filled 9×9 Sudoku
boards with unique solutions and is evaluated by exact-match accuracy of the completed grid (Wang
et al., 2025). Maze-Hard contains 30 × 30 shortest-path problems and is evaluated by exact match
to the supervised solution path (Wang et al., 2025). Because multiple optimal paths may exist in
Maze-Hard, we additionally use Maze-Unique, constructed from perfect tree-structured mazes so
that each start–goal pair has a unique solution (Kim et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2026). Mini-ARC
is a compact 5 × 5 abstract rule-induction benchmark in which the model infers a transformation
from demonstration pairs and applies it to a held-out input (Kim et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2026). We
follow the standard augmentation-based evaluation, map predictions from each augmented view back
to the original frame, aggregate identical candidate grids by voting, and report task-level Pass@1
from the highest-ranked prediction.

A.5.2    ROOT CONVERGENCE ANALYSIS
Here we provide detailed protocol for root convergence analysis used in this paper, which is used
for the local Jacobian and spectral analyses in Sections 3, 4.4, and Table 2 (a). We firstly identify
solution equilibria using Anderson acceleration with history m = 6, Tikhonov regularization 10−8 ,
and β = 1 in fp64. The solver runs for a fixed budget and returns the iterate with the smallest residual.
For FPRM, HRM, and EqR, the solved maps are one FPRM recurrent step, two HRM H-cycles, and
three EqR cycles, respectively. For QRR, Anderson acceleration is applied to the gauge-aligned map.
For each certified solution root, we run the original model from its native initialization and measure
when the trajectory reaches the corresponding root. We define τroot as the first step after which the
root-relative distance remains below δ = 10−3 for a persistence window of approximately 96 recurrent
layer-forwards. This corresponds to 48 native steps for FPRM and QRR and 4 trajectory steps for
HRM. For EqR, whose native step contains 42 layer-forwards, we use two steps, corresponding to 84
layer-forwards. Correct root found denotes the fraction of evaluation examples for which the solver
returns a certified solution equilibrium decoding to the ground truth.
To compare convergence across architectures with different computation per native recurrent step,
we report convergence times in recurrent layer-forward equivalents (LFWD). An LFWD counts one
forward evaluation of one recurrent network layer. If one native step of model m evaluates ℓm
recurrent layers, then

                                       τlfwd = ℓm τnative ,
