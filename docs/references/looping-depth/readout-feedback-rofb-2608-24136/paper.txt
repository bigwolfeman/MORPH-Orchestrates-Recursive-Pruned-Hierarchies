                                                                                             A BSTRACT
                                                 Recurrent models, which repeatedly update latent states with shared computation blocks, have
                                                 emerged as powerful architectures for solving complex reasoning tasks. Existing inference-time meth-
                                                 ods scale computation by running more steps or sampling more trajectories, but ignore information
                                                 revealed within each trajectory. Here we show that recurrent models can be improved at inference
                                                 time by using their own readout probabilities to steer latent dynamics without retraining. We intro-
                                                 duce Readout Feedback (RoFB), a test-time intervention that converts intermediate predictions into
                                                 token-wise pairwise coupling forces injected into the latent dynamics. Across three recurrent models
                                                 (AKOrN, ItrSA++, TRM) on Sudoku and Maze, RoFB yields clear gains in four of six model-task
                                                 pairs and a small gain in one. The four positive pairs achieve performance unattainable by merely
                                                 running more steps or selecting from multiple trajectories, at comparable or lower computational
                                                 cost. These results suggest that closed-loop steering of latent dynamics can serve as a complementary
                                                 inference-time control mechanism for recurrent reasoning models.

                                        1   Introduction
                                        Human experts often solve difficult problems by thinking longer: mathematicians refine intermediate structures, and
                                        chess players search and revise candidate lines before committing to a move. Modern reasoning systems increasingly
                                        exploit an analogous principle —additional computation at inference time can improve performance. In large language
                                        models, this idea appears in chain-of-thought prompting [Wei et al., 2022], self-consistency [Wang et al., 2022], and
                                        search-based reasoning [Yao et al., 2023], where inference-time computation is used to generate, evaluate, or aggregate
                                        candidate reasoning paths. These developments have motivated inference-time computation as an additional scaling
                                        axis beyond model size and training data.
                                        A complementary line of work studies recurrent reasoning models, which solve problems by repeatedly updating latent
                                        states with shared computation blocks. Unlike feedforward models with a fixed computational depth, recurrent reasoners
                                        expose inference-time computation explicitly through the number of recurrent updates. This principle has shown
                                        effective in extrapolating learned algorithms by iterating longer [Bansal et al., 2022], as well as in improving reasoning
                                        performance in language models [Yang et al., 2023, Saunshi et al., 2025, Geiping et al., 2025]. In parallel, compact
                                        recurrent reasoners designed for structured reasoning, such as HRM [Wang et al., 2025], TRM [Jolicoeur-Martineau,
                                        2025], AKOrN [Miyato et al., 2024], and related iterative transformer and self-attention models [Kubo et al., 2026]
                                        have shown strong performance on puzzle-style reasoning benchmarks including Sudoku, Maze, and ARC-AGI 1 & 2
                                        [Chollet, 2019, Chollet et al., 2026].
                                        Despite this progress, current inference-time improvements for recurrent reasoners mostly exploit their iterative structure
                                        in passive ways: running the dynamics for more steps [Wang et al., 2025, Geiping et al., 2025], or sampling multiple
                                        trajectories and selecting one according to a criterion, such as one with the lowest potential defined on the dynamics
                                        [Miyato et al., 2024], or the most confident one [Kubo et al., 2026]. Although effective, these strategies come with
                                        inherent limitations: longer trajectories may saturate or remain trapped in unsuccessful dynamical regimes, while
                                        multi-trajectory voting increases compute roughly linearly with the number of candidates. This raises a natural question:

can we improve a frozen recurrent reasoner by actively steering each latent trajectory during inference, rather than
merely running longer or sampling more trajectories?
In this work, we propose a method to steer the latent trajectories using the feedback signal derived from itself.
Specifically, we use the current readout probability vectors. We call this approach Readout Feedback (RoFB), a
closed-loop inference-time intervention for recurrent reasoning models (Fig. 1).
RoFB is motivated by an observation that tokens develop a class-dependent cluster structure in latent space when a
trajectory hits a correct solution. That is, inter-class distances become larger and the latent representations become
more clustered when the model reaches a correct solution.
RoFB is grounded in this observation and the consequent hypothesis that encouraging such inter-class separation during
inference can improve reasoning performance. RoFB, at each inference step, injects a coupling term into the latent
dynamics that encourages token inter-class separation based on the distance between tokens’ readout probabilities,
while leaving all model weights unchanged.
We evaluate RoFB on Sudoku and Maze using three complementary recurrent reasoners with distinct architectural
designs: AKOrN [Miyato et al., 2024], an oscillator-inspired model; ItrSA++ [Kubo et al., 2026], an iterative self-
attention-based model; and TRM [Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025], a compact hierarchical model. Out of the total six
model-task pairs, RoFB showed clear performance gain in four and and small but consistent gain in one, improving
performance without retraining. The former four instances are those where its gains were not reachable with the
conventional strategies of running longer or sampling more with ×10–100 efficient compute. These results suggest that
frozen recurrent reasoners contain underutilized inference-time capability that can be unlocked by closed-loop control
methods such as RoFB. Our contributions are:

      • Readout-guided inference control. We introduce RoFB, a test-time intervention that converts disagreement
        between intermediate readout probabilities into repulsive interactions between latent tokens.


      • Improved accuracy–compute trade-offs. Across three recurrent models on Sudoku and Maze, RoFB provides
        clear gains in four of six model–task pairs, reaching accuracies beyond the evaluated baseline grid at lower
        inference cost in these four pairs.


      • Mechanistic evidence and limits. Controlled interventions support a contribution of separation-related
        feedback directions to the gains. Coupling ablations suggest distinct roles for repulsion in reaching solutions
        and readout-dependent weighting in retaining them.

3     Readout Feedback (RoFB)
3.1   Geometry Matters: Inter-token Clusterization

Before describing our method RoFB, we first inspect how tokens in the latent space behave during inference. Fig. 2
shows a sample AKOrN trajectory that solves a Sudoku puzzle; Fig. 4 shows a Maze example.
In both puzzles, the total entropy decreases as the inference proceeds, indicating that the model becomes more confident
in its predictions. The entropy transition in Sudoku appears abrupt (Fig. 2, Top left), while it is more gradual in
Maze (Fig. 4, Top left). Once the entropy decreases, the solution stabilizes. In the models we examined, samples that
ultimately failed to reach the correct solution tended to have high entropy that did not fully decrease (Appendix B).
We observe that when the model reaches the solution of a problem, the latent tokens clusterize according to the classes
that they represent. That is, tokens of the same class (e.g., the same number in Sudoku, solution path in Maze) are
closely aligned in the latent space, while tokens of different classes are separated. We can see that cluster formation, or
inter-class separation, becomes more pronounced once the model reaches the correct answer (Cosine similarity and
Spherical MDS in Fig. 2). In Sudoku, almost every blank (i.e., non-input) token pair has a large similarity value ≈ 1.0
while the puzzle is still unsolved (t = 50), indicating that the tokens are aligned regardless of the classes to which

4   Related Work

Recurrent Reasoning Models Recurrent models that repeatedly apply shared computation blocks, dating back to the
Universal Transformer [Dehghani et al., 2018], have shown strong potential on algorithmic tasks [Yang et al., 2023,
Bansal et al., 2022], mathematical reasoning [Geiping et al., 2025, Bae et al., 2024, Zhu et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2026],
and puzzles including Sudoku, Maze, and ARC-AGI [Miyato et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2025, Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025,
Gao et al., 2025, Kubo et al., 2026], complementing (latent) chain-of-thought in language models [Wei et al., 2022, Hao
et al., 2024]. As an inference intervention method, Sghaier et al. [2026] proposed an inference-time mechanism that
adds on stochasticity to a frozen model and utilizes it to improve predictions. RoFB, on the other hand, leverages the
intermediate readout probabilities to steer inference, providing a deterministic and closed-loop approach for inference
intervention.

Test-time Intervention Inference-time methods for language models mostly generate, compare, or aggregate candi-
date outputs through prompting, sampling, or compute allocation [Wei et al., 2022, Zhou et al., 2022, Wang et al., 2022,
Yao et al., 2023, Zhai et al., 2026]. Methods that intervene in the computation itself use readouts to reduce predictive
entropy [Agarwal et al., 2025] or to feed probability-weighted embeddings back [Zhang et al., 2025b], steer hidden
states with online feedback from a learned verifier or from entropy and logits [Nguyen and Le, 2026, Meng et al., 2026],
or modify attention through selected heads, query/value spaces, or tokens [Zhang et al., 2023, 2025c, Torop et al., 2025,
Zhang et al., 2025a]. RoFB combines readout feedback with inter-token interaction: it converts readouts into a pairwise
coupling within the latent dynamics of recurrent reasoners, which has rarely been explored.

Dynamics and geometry of recurrent inference The internal mechanisms of recurrent models are gradually being
analyzed [Lu et al., 2025, Blayney et al., 2026, Ren and Liu, 2026]. Theoretical analyses of the internal dynamics of
recurrent Transformers include Geshkovski et al. [2023, 2024], which show the cluster-forming dynamics of recurrent
self attention dynamics. While few studies have utilized the internal mechanism in favor of improving inference, RoFB
is designed to leverage these characteristics to steer inference, which has been an open question.

Relational Structure in Output Space. Dense CRFs and CRF-RNN refine neural predictions through pairwise label
compatibility [Krähenbühl and Koltun, 2012, Zheng et al., 2015]. In source-free domain adaptation, NRC [Yang et al.,
2021] and AaD [Yang et al., 2022] exploit neighborhood consistency and attraction–dispersion objectives over predicted
distributions to update model parameters. RoFB shares the principle that output distributions contain useful relational
structure, but uses the readout probabilities of a recurrent reasoning model at inference time, which makes it novel.

5     Experiments

We evaluate RoFB on two benchmarks, Sudoku and Maze, with the three models described earlier.
Sudoku is a number-placement puzzle, whose objective is to fill a 9 × 9 grid with digits so that each column, row, and
box (3 × 3 subgrid) contains all of the digits from 1 to 9. Following the prior works [Wang et al., 2025, Kubo et al.,
2026], we used Sudoku Extreme dataset, and applied the same preprocessing and augmentation techniques. We tested
with 20,000 puzzle boards randomly sampled from all test boards.
Maze is a pathfinding problem where the goal is to find a shortest path from a starting point to a target point in a 30 × 30
grid environment with obstacles. Following the prior works [Wang et al., 2025, Kubo et al., 2026], we used Maze Hard
dataset, and applied the same preprocessing techniques. We tested with all 1,000 puzzle boards.

Training. For AKOrN and ItrSA++, we trained the models on each task using truncated backpropagation through time
[Aicher et al., 2019] following Kubo et al. [2026]. For TRM, we trained the model using truncated backpropagation
through time with deep supervision, following Jolicoeur-Martineau [2025]; see Appendix D.

Evaluation. For AKOrN and ItrSA++, we evaluated the performance of a model based on the readout after Teval ∈
{64, 128, 256} recurrence steps. We also evaluated model performances using confidence-based voting [Kubo et al.,
2026] with Kvote ∈ {1, 4, 16, 64}, where trajectories are sampled with random initial values and the one minimizing
the sum of entropy over all cells is selected as the final output.
For TRM, we evaluated Nblock ∈ {8, 12, 16} outer iterations, using 16 as the standard horizon following Jolicoeur-
Martineau [2025]. We did not apply voting for TRM, as TRM learns a fixed initial value of the latent dynamics, rather
than sampling it from a distribution, and thus the voting procedure is not applicable.
For Sudoku, we report board accuracy, where a prediction is correct only if all cells match the ground-truth board. For
Maze, we report shortest-path accuracy, where a prediction is correct only if the predicted path is the shortest path (not
necessarily the given ground-truth path).

Hyperparameter Tuning.        Hyperparameters are tuned on a validation set; see Appendix E.

Additional Computational Cost of RoFB. Since RoFB increases the computational cost per inference step, we
evaluate performance based on FLOPs at each Kvote and Teval value for a fair comparison. The increase in compute per
trajectory and per step due to RoFB was kept small, within +20% (see Appendix Table 4). We also provide results
based on wall time, including hyperparameter search, in the Appendix E, F. Since hyperparameter search is performed
only once, the computational efficiency of RoFB improves as Kvote and Teval increase.

for TRM on Maze, where both saturate, accuracy generally increases with Kvote and Teval for both.

Performance Gain of RoFB. We observe a clear performance boost with RoFB in four of the six model-task pairs
(AKOrN on both tasks, ItrSA++ on Maze, and TRM on Sudoku) at the same or lower computational cost, while the gain
was small in ItrSA++ on Sudoku (+0.6%) and absent in TRM on Maze (Fig. 3). Crucially, in the four positive model-
task pairs, RoFB reaches operating points that are unattainable by either prolonging Teval or increasing Kvote in the
baseline. For instance, on Maze, ItrSA++ with RoFB achieves 83.4% accuracy at (Kvote , Teval ) = (1, 128), exceeding
the best baseline accuracy of 82.1% across the evaluated grid, attained at (64, 128), while using approximately 1/62 of
its inference FLOPs. For TRM on Sudoku, where confidence-based voting is unavailable, RoFB lifts board accuracy
from 68.4% to 74.2% (+5.8%) at Nblock =16, demonstrating that the gain does not depend on the availability of a voting
mechanism.

5.2   Mechanistic Analysis

We next ask how RoFB produces these gains. We find that (i) the gains require the separation-related directions of the
feedback, (ii) the feedback plays two roles—repulsion that helps trajectories reach a solution and readout-dependent
weighting that helps retain it—and (iii) among the other separation interventions we tested, some also help but none
outperforms RoFB. We then characterize when RoFB does not help. Details and statistics are in Appendices I–K.

Separation-related directions are required. As intended, RoFB increases inter-class separation in all six model-task
pairs, including instances that remain incorrect under both conditions (Tables 9 and 10). To test whether this change
matters for accuracy, we remove from the RoFB force its projection onto the direction that changes inter-class similarity
to first order and compare with RoFB rescaled to the same magnitude. Removal lowers accuracy by 2.3–3.3% on
Sudoku for all three models and by 8.9 and 11.0% for AKOrN and ItrSA++ on Maze, with no resolved change on TRM
on Maze (Table 11).

Two roles: reaching and retaining a solution. The coupling 1 − p(i)⊤ p(j) has two limits: when readouts are
uncertain it is nearly constant, so RoFB reduces to uniform repulsion among blank tokens; when readouts are confident
it vanishes within a predicted class and remains repulsive across classes. Replacing it by constant repulsion (h ≡ −1,
same gate and strength) separates these roles (Appendix J). On Sudoku, constant repulsion matches RoFB (AKOrN 91.6
vs. 91.5%, TRM 74.7 vs. 74.2%, ItrSA++ 77.0 vs. 77.0%; Table 13), whereas norm-matched random directions fall
4–11% short of RoFB in three pairs. Where unsolved states show nearly parallel blank tokens (Fig. 2, t = 50), repulsion
itself thus suffices to reach solutions. On Maze, constant repulsion falls short (AKOrN 73.8 vs. 78.8%, ItrSA++ 84.3
vs. 85.3%). In longer runs, constant and readout-dependent coupling reach a correct solution in a similar number of
trajectories, but among those that do, 11.4% vs. 3.1% (AKOrN) and 2.0% vs. 0.2% (ItrSA++) later end incorrectly.
Readout dependence therefore matters for retaining a solution, consistent with the vanishing within-class repulsion
once predictions are confident.

Comparison with other separation interventions. Inter-class separation could also be promoted in other ways.
To examine whether such alternatives are effective as well, we replaced only the direction of the RoFB force with
forces derived from three readout-based clustering objectives (margin repulsion, a probability-weighted contrastive
loss, and within-class variance plus a centroid margin) and from a readout-free K-means attraction, each with a
validation-selected strength (Appendix I). Some of these alternatives also improved accuracy over the no-feedback
baseline: margin repulsion, which, like RoFB, repels tokens with dissimilar predictions, did so in four model-task
pairs (by 2.2–7.6%), and the other two readout-based objectives did so on AKOrN on Maze. None of them, however,
was significantly more accurate than RoFB in any of the 24 comparisons, whereas 12 were significantly less accurate
(Fig. 14). In the settings we examined, RoFB therefore performed best among these separation interventions. Separately,
a fixed-scale entropy-gradient control changed accuracy by at most 0.7% in the four pairs where RoFB gained 3.2–7.2%
(Appendix K), suggesting that RoFB does more than sharpen predictions.

When does RoFB not help? On TRM on Maze, RoFB increased inter-class separation without improving accuracy.
Two possible reasons for the this non-effectiveness are given. Frist, incorrect predictions were already confident and
strongly separated, weakening feedback on confident errors through the entropy gate: 71 of 72 incorrect outputs have
maximum normalized entropy at most 0.10, and all 72 have a silhouette score of at least 0.5 (Appendix B). Second,
because the coupling acts on current predicted classes, it strengthens the relations implied by these predictions but

does not by itself distinguish wrong assignments from correct ones. We also examined TRM on ARC-AGI-1 [Chollet,
2019], including harder logical puzzles. The validation-selected RoFB configuration showed no consistent pass@1
improvement on the remaining 300 evaluation puzzles. In a separate diagnostic sample balanced between baseline
successes and failures, 45 of 97 confident terminal predictions were incorrect (Appendix M). Thus, confidence and
prediction-based separation do not guarantee correctness, although their role in the lack of improvement remains a
hypothesis.

6   Limitations and Discussion
We do not yet have a validated diagnostic that predicts whether RoFB will improve accuracy on a new model or task.
Confidence and class separation can both be high for incorrect predictions, as observed in TRM on Maze, so neither
alone establishes when feedback will be beneficial.
Our main evaluation covers two puzzle benchmarks (Sudoku Extreme, Maze Hard) and three small recurrent archi-
tectures, with an additional TRM experiment on ARC-AGI-1. We do not evaluate language models, to which direct
transfer is non-trivial. If RoFB is applied directly to LLMs, the large vocabulary size may dilute the inter-token coupling
on which RoFB relies. Whether RoFB or such a variant provides additional gains to LLMs is left to future work.

7   Conclusions
We proposed RoFB to enhance the inference performance of recurrent reasoning models. RoFB utilizes the readout
probabilities during inference to provide feedback to the latent states. Across three architectures on Sudoku and Maze,
RoFB yielded clear gains in four of six model–task pairs and a small gain in one, reaching accuracies unattainable by
longer rollouts or confidence-based voting at lower inference compute. Our analysis indicates two roles of the feedback,
repulsion that helps trajectories leave unsolved states and readout-dependent weighting that retains solutions once
reached. Our results provide evidence that readout-based closed-loop steering can complement existing inference-time
strategies such as longer rollouts and multi-trajectory voting. While the present evidence is limited to puzzle-style
benchmarks and compact recurrent reasoners, it suggests a promising direction for using intermediate predictions to
control frozen recurrent dynamics at test time.

the three models and the Sudoku and Maze tasks. In a nutshell, we show
       • Correct solutions tend to exhibit strong class-aligned clustering of latent tokens and high confidence.
       • In the Sudoku task, strong clusterization and high confidence are strong indicators of correctness.
       • In the Maze task, strong clusterization and high confidence do not necessarily imply correctness. Both

B.2                           Confidence and Correctness

The relationship between confidence and correctness follows a similar pattern. Correct solutions tend to have high
confidence, but high confidence does not necessarily imply correctness. In Sudoku, Figure 7 shows a clear separation

Confidence therefore almost perfectly ranks correct above incorrect outputs in this sample, while still allowing
exceptions.
In Maze, the correct- and incorrect-output distributions overlap more, especially for TRM. The corresponding AUROCs
are 0.788, 0.822, and 0.607 for AKOrN, ItrSA++, and TRM, respectively. These results show that the models can be
confident in incorrect Maze solutions; for TRM, confidence offers only limited discrimination between correct and
incorrect outputs.

B.4                    TRM on Maze

TRM on Maze illustrates the limits of both clustering and confidence as indicators of correctness. The median silhouette
scores are 0.970 and 0.968 for correct and incorrect outputs, respectively, while the corresponding median maximum
normalized entropies are 9.247 × 10−31 and 1.767 × 10−30 . Of the 72 incorrect outputs, 71 have hmax ≤ 0.10 and
all 72 have a silhouette score of at least 0.5. Incorrect predictions can therefore be both strongly clustered and highly
confident. The AUROCs above indicate a difference in ranking, but not a clear separation of the two groups.
This pattern provides a possible explanation for the limited benefit of RoFB on TRM on Maze. RoFB promotes
separation according to predicted classes, so increasing separation need not repair an already well-separated but
incorrect assignment. Its entropy gate also reduces feedback when predictions are confident; if confident errors arise
during the intervention window, uncertainty alone may fail to identify them as requiring intervention. These observations
are consistent with the finding that increased inter-class separation does not yield an accuracy gain on TRM on Maze.
However, the present analysis measures ordinary-inference terminal states, so it does not establish when these errors
become confident or whether gate suppression causes the lack of improvement.

D    Experimental Setup

Tasks and datasets. We evaluate three iterative reasoners — AKOrN [Miyato et al., 2024], ItrSA++ [Kubo et al.,
2026], and TRM [Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025] — on two combinatorial reasoning tasks: Sudoku Extreme (9×9 grid,
1,000 base puzzles augmented to ∼1,001,000 training samples; 422,786 test puzzles) and Maze Hard (30×30 grid,
1,000 train / 1,000 test). Both datasets are shared across all three models — the same training and test splits, with
model-specific token vocabularies — so that any across-model gap reflects architecture and training choices, not data.

Training protocol. All three models are trained from scratch with AdamW [Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017], truncated
backpropagation through time (TBPTT) [Aicher et al., 2019, Kubo et al., 2026], where the backpropagation gradients
are computed only on the last fixed number of steps. The parameters were renewed with an exponential moving average
(EMA). We follow each model’s published recipe for the model-specific hyperparameters. Full hyperparameters are in
Appendix N (Tables 19–21).


E    Hyperparameter Search for RoFB

For each model-task pair, we perform a single hyperparameter sweep over a scaling parameter λ and three RoFB gate
parameters α, tmin , τ defined in Eqs. (8) and (10). Beyond these four parameters, we fixed the coupling function to
h(d) = −d, representing repulsive feedback, which is held constant across the tasks and models.
We used Optuna [Akiba et al., 2019] with the TPE sampler and additionally enable the Successive Halving Pruner [Li
et al., 2018] (an ASHA-style pruner with reduction factor η = 3 and minresource = 1) for all model-task pairs, running
ntrial = 30 trials per pair. The pruner reports intermediate validation accuracy after each of nchunks disjoint chunks of
the validation set: nchunks = 4 for Sudoku tasks (Nval = 500, i.e. 125 boards per chunk) and nchunks = 2 for Maze tasks
(Nval = 100, i.e. 50 boards per chunk). Each trial evaluates a single (λ, α, tmin , τ ) tuple by running inference with
RoFB on the full validation set at a single operating point (Kvote = 1 and Teval matching the standard evaluation horizon
per model: 256 Kuramoto steps for AKOrN, 256 self-attention steps (= 64 outer blocks) for ItrSA++, 16 outer steps for
TRM), and computes the aggregated accuracy over all validation boards (board accuracy for Sudoku; shortest path
accuracy for Maze). The validation set Vaug consists of the first Nval boards of the training set, with one task-specific
symmetry-group augmentation applied per board. The best tuple is selected by the aggregated validation accuracy and
is then reused at every (K, T ) operating point in Fig. 3, treating the sweep as a one-time per-cell compute cost that we
account for separately in Section F.
tmin is reported in the sweep-internal time unit (Kuramoto steps for AKOrN; self-attention steps for ItrSA++; L-level
steps for TRM, where one TRM outer step = 18 L-level steps on Sudoku and 12 on Maze).

F    Computational cost of RoFB
Here we explain the additional compute introduced by RoFB at test time, relative to a baseline forward pass. The two
sources of overhead are (i) the one-time hyperparameter sweep and (ii) the per-step RoFB block inserted into every test
forward pass. The former is a fixed cost across all (K, T ) operating points, while the latter scales with the baseline
compute and shifts the RoFB curve to the right by a constant factor on a log-compute axis.

(trajectories per board) × (steps per trajectory), normalized so that the baseline operating point (Kvote =1, T =Tmax )
without RoFB equals 1. Under this normalization, the cost decomposes into a per-inference forward term and a
one-time-per-cell hyperparameter-sweep term:

H    Hyperparameter Sensitivity
In this section, we examine whether the gains from RoFB persist when its hyperparameters are changed. The results
depend on the model, task, and parameter:

       • Positive gains were robust to the tested hyperparameter changes in AKOrN on Maze, ItrSA++ on Maze, and
         TRM on Sudoku, remaining positive at every tested endpoint.
       • Some model–task pairs show hyperparameter sensitivity: AKOrN on Sudoku can fall below its baseline, and
         ItrSA++ on Maze can lose most of its gain when the entropy threshold is increased.
       • No improvement was confirmed at the reference settings for ItrSA++ on Sudoku or TRM on Maze.

We describe the evaluation settings and summarize the sensitivity of each model–task pair in Fig. 13.

Evaluation setting. We vary the feedback strength λ, intervention start time tmin , entropy threshold α, and gate
temperature τ one at a time around a fixed reference tuple for each model–task pair. All evaluations use repulsive,
entropy-gated RoFB; ItrSA++ uses projected feedback.
Each model–task pair uses a fixed checkpoint, one trajectory per board, and paired inputs across conditions. The
inference seed is 42 for the stochastic models; TRM uses its learned deterministic initialization. AKOrN runs for 256
recurrent updates, ItrSA++ for 64 outer blocks containing 256 self-attention updates, and TRM for 16 outer iterations.
We evaluate five values per parameter on 500 validation boards per pair, and the prespecified lowest, reference, and
highest values on 20,000 Sudoku or 1,000 Maze test boards. For each model–task pair, we perform four one-at-a-time
sweeps, one for each hyperparameter, while keeping the other three fixed. The reference-setting evaluation is shared
across these sweeps. The validation boards use training-derived augmentations. Table 8 gives the perturbation ranges: a
star denotes the reference value, and d is the integer spacing between adjacent start times. The other three parameters
are held fixed, except that AKOrN on Sudoku uses rounded center values and full-precision fixed coordinates at the
endpoints.
For the start-time grid, (t∗min , d) is (16, 8) and (128, 26) for AKOrN on Sudoku and Maze, (64, 26) and (104, 26) for
ItrSA++, and (88, 29) and (88, 19) for TRM. ItrSA++ permits feedback at one-based update t > tmin through update
256. The spacing d is set to 10% of the total update horizon, rounded to the nearest integer, and reduced if needed so
that two earlier and two later settings fit between zero and the last possible start time. For AKOrN on Sudoku, the
reference start at update 16 restricts the spacing to 8; the other pairs use the rounded 10% spacing.

Controls. We compare each setting with a no-feedback baseline on the same boards. For ItrSA++, the test comparisons
in Fig. 13 use the same update procedure with feedback strength set to zero and the reference start time fixed; its
validation comparisons use the ordinary model. We additionally check a no-feedback control matched to each
intervention start time and the ordinary model, because the extra normalization in the feedback procedure can slightly
change the numerical trajectory even when feedback strength is zero.

Metrics and uncertainty. We report the difference in accuracy between RoFB and its corresponding no-feedback
baseline in percentage points, using exact-solution accuracy for Sudoku and shortest-path accuracy for Maze. Pointwise
95% confidence intervals use 100,000 paired bootstrap draws over the same test problems. These intervals describe
uncertainty for a fixed checkpoint and inference condition and are not corrected for multiple comparisons.
For each hyperparameter, we check whether the confidence intervals at the lower, reference, and upper settings are all
above zero.

Improvements persist across multiple settings. AKOrN on Maze, ItrSA++ on Maze, and TRM on Sudoku retain
positive gains at all eight tested endpoints, with pointwise 95% confidence intervals entirely above zero. Fig. 13 shows
the gains at the lower, reference, and upper settings for each hyperparameter and all six model–task pairs.

I     Mechanistic Analysis of RoFB (1): Inter-class Separation

In this subsection, we provide a detailed mechanistic analysis of how RoFB influences the latent space and model
trajectories.


I.1   RoFB promotes inter-class separation.

We quantify inter-class separation using the same metrics, cosine gap ∆cos = Csame − Cdiff and the cosine-distance
silhouette score, described in Appendix B. Csame and Cdiff are the mean cosine similarities between distinct cells with
the same and different reference labels, respectively. We use reference digits in Sudoku and reference path versus
non-path labels in Maze, restricting the analysis to blank Sudoku cells and Maze passage cells excluding the start and
goal. Larger values of either index indicate stronger separation. For silhouette, we average only over cells with defined
comparisons, excluding singleton classes, and use a denominator floor of 10−12 ; Appendix B instead assigns singleton
cells zero contribution and retains them in the averaging denominator.
We compare terminal latent states under RoFB and the no-force reference on 2,000 Sudoku instances and 900 Maze
instances per model, with one candidate per instance and matched initial states. The standard inference horizon is
256 updates for AKOrN, 256 self-attention updates (64 blocks) for ItrSA++, and 16 outer iterations for TRM. RoFB
replays the gate sequence recorded from the no-force reference. Reference labels are used for geometry analysis, not by
standard RoFB. Reported changes are RoFB minus the no-force reference; geometry values are dimensionless, and 95%
confidence intervals (CIs) use 20,000 paired bootstrap resamples of instances.
Both indices increased with RoFB in all six model–task pairs when averaged over all instances (Table 9). These averages
include instances that were correct under both conditions, incorrect under both conditions, or changed correctness
between conditions.
To examine whether the aggregate increase was explained solely by a larger fraction of correct instances, we restricted
the same runs and evaluation settings used in Table 9 to instances that were incorrect under both RoFB and the no-force
reference. Correctness requires agreement with the reference solution in Sudoku and a valid shortest path in Maze,
including alternative shortest paths. For each model–task pair, we recomputed the mean changes and conditional 95%
CIs by resampling paired instances within this subset; the subset sizes are reported in Table 10. Both indices increased
in all six pairs, with positive 95% CIs throughout. Table 10 reports these subset averages, whereas Table 9 reports
averages over all instances.
The increases among instances that remained incorrect show that improved separation was not confined to instances
that became correct. These correctness groups are defined after intervention and provide descriptive stratification, rather
than causal adjustment for correctness. Separation also does not guarantee a correct solution: TRM on Maze showed
increases in both geometry indices while retaining the same instance-level correctness as the no-force reference.

Ablating inter-class separation component hinders performance. To see the effect of inter-class separation on
performance gain, we next performed inference with RoFB while removing the component of the gradient of inter-class
similarity. Specifically, we removed the projection of the feedback force onto the direction that changes Cdiff to first
order.

I.3   Why Inter-class Separation Induces Solution

Why does inter-class separation induce a solution? We speculate that the learned recurrent dynamics already contain
task-specific information that supports solution finding, and that RoFB can help these dynamics by reinforcing separation
between cells representing different classes. RoFB leaves the learned update rule and its parameters unchanged while
adding feedback to the evolving state. In this view, the feedback facilitates the use of structure acquired during training,
rather than supplying a solution independently of the learned dynamics.

The coupling provides one possible explanation. If all readouts are uniform over C classes, then 1−p(i)⊤ p(j) = 1−1/C,

repulsion between classes.
This distinction may matter more when many cell pairs share a predicted class. At the standard horizon, the observed

L    Natural Frequencies and the Original AKOrN Architecture
RoFB does not require removing AKOrN’s natural-frequency term. We evaluated models trained with and without this
term in both our modified architecture (Eq. (3)) and a reproduction of the original Sudoku architecture [Miyato et al.,
2024]. The latter retains the original four-dimensional oscillators, geometric-transform attention, and rotation-invariant
readout. RoFB was applied only at inference, with three independently trained checkpoints per condition.
For the modified architecture, we retained the four-channel spherical grouping and evaluated one fixed RoFB configura-
tion on 20,000 Sudoku Extreme test boards at 256 recurrent steps. For the original architecture, we trained on 9,000
SATNet boards, selected one shared RoFB configuration across the two natural-frequency conditions on 1,000 RRN
validation boards, and evaluated on 18,000 disjoint RRN test boards at 128 steps. Both experiments used one trajectory
per board and compared RoFB with the corresponding baseline at the same recurrent horizon. Training recipes and
evaluation sets differ across architectures; the relevant comparison is therefore within each architecture.
With natural frequencies enabled, all three gains were positive in both architectures. Without them, gains were mixed
in the modified architecture, while the original-architecture reproduction showed no improvement: all three within-
checkpoint paired 95% intervals included zero. Thus, our results do not support attributing RoFB’s benefit to the removal
of natural frequencies, but they also do not establish robustness across all training conditions. The improvement on the
original architecture required validation-based adjustment; directly transferring the modified model’s configuration
reduced accuracy by 31.7–48.2 percentage points on average across training seeds. These results are limited to Sudoku
and do not establish the same behavior on Maze.

M      RoFB on TRM for ARC-AGI-1
We evaluated test-time RoFB on ARC-AGI-1 using the public TRM checkpoint [ARC Prize Foundation, 2025], without
retraining. The intervention uses repulsive coupling and a warmup followed by a soft maximum-cell-entropy gate, as in
Eq. (10); for this experiment, the gate is recomputed once per outer iteration. Of the 400 evaluation puzzles, 100 were
used to select (λ, α, tmin , τ ) by validation pass@1 using Optuna’s TPE sampler, and the remaining 300 were used for
evaluation. The selected configuration, (0.0533, 0.0407, 13, 0.0114), was reused across augmentation settings, with
feedback active during outer iterations 14–16. This historical sweep completed 56 trials and used 20 augmentations for
validation; its split and search protocol differ from the main experiments.
Table 18 shows small, mixed changes across augmentation settings. At 100 augmentations, pass@1, pass@5, and
pass@100 are unchanged. These point estimates from one checkpoint do not establish a consistent accuracy gain.
A separate diagnostic subset of 40 puzzles, balanced between baseline successes and failures, suggests a limitation of
prediction-based coupling. With four augmented inputs per puzzle and no feedback, 45 of the 97 terminal predictions
that were confident (maximum input-cell entropy at most 10−3 ) were incorrect, and 36 of these 45 did not change
during the final eight high-level updates. Without feedback, cell errors concentrate where the output should differ
from the input (35.0%, versus 1.8% on cells that should be copied, for matching input/output shapes). An erroneous
cell’s predicted-color group nevertheless contains mostly correct cells (median 72%). The coupling therefore does not
explicitly distinguish incorrect assignments from correct peers sharing the same confident prediction. This is consistent
with limited benefit, but does not establish the cause of the small gains or imply that such errors cannot be corrected.
