                                                                                                                     Abstract
                                                         Diffusion language models (DLMs) offer parallel sampling for faster generation than autoregressive
                                                         models, an increasingly important advantage as inference becomes a dominant computational
                                                         workload. However, DLMs degrade quickly with fewer sampling steps, process the full sequence
                                                         at every step, and require many passes over the dataset to reach good quality. We introduce
                                                         Next-Concept Modeling (NCM), a two-stage, coarse-to-fine framework: a multiscale residual
                                                         VQ-VAE encodes text into a hierarchy of discrete codes, and a transformer generates one level
                                                         at a time, predicting all codes within a level in parallel. At matched parameter scales, NCM
                                                         achieves better generative perplexity than the strongest DLM baseline on LM1B while running
                                                         21× faster. On OpenWebText, NCM is competitive with the strongest DLM while running 10×
                                                         faster; on XSum, it matches the strongest DLM’s ROUGE-L while being 31× faster. On the
                                                         reasoning task of Sudoku, NCM outperforms autoregressive and diffusion baselines across all
                                                         difficulty levels, using fewer function evaluations.

1      Introduction
Diffusion language models (DLMs) are the leading non-autoregressive language modeling paradigm
[1, 2, 3]. Their main appeal is their ability to produce many tokens at a time through iterative
denoising, which can make generation substantially faster than in autoregressive (AR) models
that generate one token at a time. This is becoming increasingly important, as language model
inference is one of the dominant computational workloads [4, 5]. DLMs are moving from research to
practice: open models such as LLaDA [6] scale to billions of parameters, commercial systems such
as Mercury [7] and DiffusionGemma [8] serve diffusion models in production, and diffusion drafters
like DFlash [9] accelerate AR models through speculative decoding.
The promise of DLMs is parallel sampling: generating a sequence in far fewer sequential steps than
it has tokens. In practice however:

• (i) Many sampling steps. Diffusion models predict tokens in parallel but still perform successive
  denoising passes. Reducing these passes can substantially degrade generation quality (Figure 5),
  limiting the speed gains from parallelism.
• (ii) Expensive sampling steps. Processing the full sequence at every step limits speedups, and
  bidirectional attention prevents direct reuse of AR-style KV caching [10, 11].
• (iii) Large training budgets. DLM training often involves many passes over the same data.
  For example, strong diffusion baselines such as DUO and MDLM are trained on OpenWebText
  for 1M steps, processing over 500B tokens [12, 3, 13].

Prior work reduces the number of sampling steps mainly through few-step samplers and distillation,
either by distilling a many-step teacher into a few-step student [12, 14] or by learning a direct map
between noise levels [15]. These approaches come at the cost of additional training stages on top of
an already expensive model. Few-step sampling also comes at a price: distilled models tend to be
less diverse than the models they are distilled from. On standard benchmarks such as OpenWebText
[16] and LM1B [17], they reach a lower generative perplexity at the expense of sample diversity, as
measured by mean per-sample unigram entropy [15]. Moreover, these approaches leave the per-step

cost unchanged. Block-autoregressive diffusion enables KV caching at the cost of parallelism: tokens
within each block can be denoised in parallel, but blocks must be generated sequentially, with each
waiting for the previous block to finish [18, 19].
We introduce Next-Concept Modeling (NCM), a two-stage language modeling framework. A
multiscale residual VQ-VAE [20] encodes text into K levels of discrete codes, from short coarse
representations to longer fine ones. A transformer generates these levels sequentially, predicting
all codes within each level in parallel, following next-scale prediction for images [21, 22]. NCM
achieves (i) high generation quality and diversity in K ≪ L steps without distillation (Figure 5);
(ii) lower sampling cost by processing short code sequences at early levels (Figure 1); and (iii)
strong performance with fewer training passes over the data (Figure 7).
We evaluate NCM models at approximately 100M parameters against discrete-diffusion, flow-
based, and hybrid baselines, following standard evaluation protocols [3, 12]. Across unconditional
generation, conditional generation (translation and summarization), and reasoning tasks (Sudoku),
we find:
    • (i) High quality in few steps. Across three unconditional generation datasets (TinyStories,
      LM1B, and OpenWebText), NCM matches or outperforms strong diffusion baselines with
      approximately 4–11× fewer function evaluations (NFE), while maintaining sample entropy
      close to the data (Figure 5). On conditional generation (XSum summarization and WMT14
      translation), it uses approximately 6–16× fewer NFE than unguided baselines, achieving
      the best summarization scores and outperforming all but one non-AR translation baseline
      (Table 2). On Sudoku, NCM outperforms AR and diffusion baselines across difficulty levels
      while using fewer NFE than any of them (Table 3).
    • (ii) Fast inference and effective test-time scaling. Fewer sampling steps and lower
      per-step cost from shorter coarse levels yield 10–31× faster generation at comparable or better
      quality (Figures 1 and 5). Using the saved time to generate more candidates and select among
      them with MBR decoding yields about 7% higher ROUGE-L than the strongest summarization
      baseline within the same sampling-time budget (Figure 6).
    • (iii) Lower training budgets. NCM achieves competitive generation quality with approxi-
      mately 4–5× fewer training tokens than strong diffusion baselines on LM1B and OpenWebText,
      including both tokenizer and generator training (Figure 7; Appendix C).
We also show preliminary scaling results: increasing NCM’s model size improves generation quality
on LM1B at a fixed training-token budget (Figure 6a). Qualitative probes suggest that coarse levels
capture global structure while finer levels add detail (Appendix H, Figures 17–19).


2    Background & Related Work
Diffusion & Flow-Based Models for Text Generation. Text generation using Diffusion &
Flow-Based Models is an iterative refinement process. Discrete methods operate on token sequences
[1, 2, 3]; continuous methods evolve representations of tokens in an embedding space [13, 23, 24, 25].
Distillation/flow-map techniques enable reduction of the number of sampling steps [12, 14, 15];
however, full-sequence formulations still require processing a length-L representation in every step.
Block-Autoregressive Diffusion allows KV caching through sequential block generation: tokens within
a block are generated in parallel; however, each block must be generated after the previous blocks
[18, 19]. NCM modifies both the representation and the generation schedule. NCM generates a

Discrete latent variables and next scale prediction. A number of image generative models
use discrete tokenizers [20, 26, 27]. Residual quantization corrects for errors made at previous steps
and uses those errors to create a new codebook [28] which has been adapted to neural audio codecs
[29, 30]. Next-scale prediction generates such hierarchies coarse to fine, one scale at a time in parallel,
for fast, high-quality images [21, 22]. NCM extends residual quantization and next-scale prediction
to text through a hierarchy of discrete codes that determines the generation order. While latent
diffusion models generate continuous representations of text [31, 32], NCM produces successive
discrete levels. Adapting this approach to text requires learning useful coarse representations without
a natural counterpart to image downsampling. Accurate reconstruction is also important: a single
token error can alter meaning, prevent code from running, or invalidate a Sudoku solution.

Hierarchical and concept-level text generation. Several approaches organize language
modeling around larger units and learned abstractions. MEGABYTE [33] combines global modeling
of byte patches with local byte prediction, while H-Net [34] learns dynamic chunks within a multistage
hierarchy. Large Concept Models [35] predict sentence representations. ConceptLM [36] predicts
discrete concepts to guide subsequent token generation, and Dynamic Large Concept Models [37]
learn variable-length concepts for computation in a compressed representation space. HDLM [38]
progressively refines tokens through a hierarchy of semantic vocabularies. NCM represents an
entire sequence through discrete levels of increasing resolution and generates these levels in order
using a shared transformer. Predicting all codes within each level in parallel gives a short generation
path, while the shorter coarse levels reduce computation during early steps.

3       Next-Concept Modeling
NCM generates text through a learned coarse-to-fine hierarchy of discrete codes. We first train a
multiscale residual VQ tokenizer to represent text at progressively finer levels of detail, then freeze
it and train a transformer to generate the hierarchy. At each level, the transformer predicts all
codes in parallel, conditioned on the cumulative reconstruction of earlier levels. The tokenizer’s
decoder maps the completed reconstruction back to text. Figure 3 previews this process through a
German–English translation, showing how the output develops across levels. The term concept here
refers to the information carried by the coarse levels of the hierarchy.

Conditional generation. NCM supports prompt-conditioned generation through a prefix-LM
attention pattern. Prompt tokens are embedded using the frozen VQ-VAE encoder’s token embedding
table and projected into the hidden space. Prompt positions attend to each other; each target block
attends to the prompt and itself, while prompt positions cannot attend to target blocks.

4     Experiments
We evaluate NCM along four axes:
    • Quality. We first assess tokenizer quality through reconstruction accuracy and codebook
      utilization (§4.1). We then evaluate unconditional generation on TinyStories [40], One Billion
      Word (LM1B) [17], and OpenWebText (OWT) [16], measuring generative perplexity alongside
      sample diversity (§4.2). For conditional generation, we evaluate WMT14 German–English
      translation [41] using BLEU [42, 43] and XSum summarization [44] using ROUGE-1/2/L [45]
      (§4.3).
    • Efficiency. We compare the number of function evaluations (NFE) and wall-clock time
      required to achieve a given generation quality, as well as the training-token budgets used to
      obtain these results (§4.6).
    • Reasoning. We compare NCM with autoregressive and diffusion baselines on Sudoku, where
      successful generation requires satisfying interdependent constraints across the entire output
      (§4.4).
    • Scaling. We investigate how scaling the model size improves generation quality and whether
      NCM’s fast sampling can translate into further quality gains through additional test-time
      compute (§4.5).
Across these evaluations, NCM achieves competitive generation quality with substantially lower
sampling cost. We train a separate tokenizer and generator for each dataset. Unless otherwise
stated, our generators have approximately 100M parameters, comparable to or smaller than the
baselines. We follow established evaluation protocols from prior diffusion language modeling work
where possible. Appendices B–E provide dataset, architecture, training, and baseline details, along
with additional results.

4.5                Scaling
We study how NCM benefits from additional compute through larger models and increased inference
budgets. We evaluate model scaling by increasing generator size and test-time scaling by selecting
among multiple candidates using minimum Bayes risk (MBR) decoding.

Model Scaling. Scaling NCM’s model size improves both generation quality and sample diversity
at a fixed training-token budget (Figure 6a). We train generators with 97M, 317M, and 725M
parameters on LM1B, all sharing the same frozen tokenizer. These results suggest that NCM scales
effectively, with potential for further gains from larger generators.

Test-Time Scaling. In Figure 1 and Table 2, we show that NCM matches or outperforms
diffusion baselines while using significantly fewer function evaluations (NFEs) and less wall-clock
time. A natural question is whether we can convert these efficiency gains into further improvements
in quality. We generate multiple candidates and use minimum Bayes risk (MBR) decoding [51] to
select the candidate with the highest average similarity to the others. On XSum, Figure 6b shows
that NCM improves over diffusion baselines at matched inference time.

4.6                Efficiency
Inference Efficiency. In Figure 5, we show that NCM achieves lower or comparable generative
perplexity than the strongest diffusion baselines using far fewer function evaluations (NFE). However,
NFE alone does not capture the full inference efficiency picture, since the cost per step differs across
different diffusion language models; many DLMs process the full sequence at every step and use
samplers that can introduce additional overhead. NCM, on the other hand, processes very few
tokens at the coarse levels of its hierarchy, saving compute at these early steps. We benchmark
end-to-end wall-clock time for generating 1,024 sequences at batch size 16 on a single NVIDIA H100
SXM 80GB GPU (Figure 1). On LM1B, NCM uses 11× fewer NFE than the strongest diffusion
language model baseline, DUO, and runs 21× faster at better generative perplexity. On XSum, it
uses 16× fewer NFE than the strongest diffusion baseline, AURORA-LM, and runs 31× faster at

5    Conclusion
We introduce Next-Concept Modeling (NCM), a two-stage framework that first learns a discrete
coarse-to-fine representation of text and then trains a transformer to generate its levels in order.
Predicting all codes within each level in parallel provides a short sequential generation path. On
unconditional generation across TinyStories, LM1B, and OpenWebText, NCM achieves competitive
or better Gen. PPL than strong diffusion baselines with approximately 4–11× fewer function
evaluations (NFE), while maintaining sample entropy close to the data. Generation is 10–21×
faster on LM1B and OpenWebText. On conditional generation, NCM uses approximately 6–16×
fewer NFE than unguided diffusion baselines on WMT14 translation and XSum summarization,
achieving competitive translation quality and the best summarization scores. On XSum, it runs
31× faster than the strongest diffusion baseline. It also uses approximately 4–5× fewer training
tokens than strong diffusion baselines on LM1B and OpenWebText, including both tokenizer and
generator training. Finally, preliminary scaling results on LM1B show that increasing the generator
size improves generation quality at a fixed training-token budget.

I     Limitations & Future Work
NCM shows promising results on unconditional generation and downstream conditional generation
tasks such as summarization and translation. Several directions remain for further improvement.
NCM fixes both the hierarchy depth K and the per-level lengths ℓk , so even short continuations
traverse the full hierarchy; an input-dependent schedule would adapt compute to example complexity.
NCM relies on a separately trained multiscale VQ-VAE tokenizer, decoupling representation learning
from the generative objective. An end-to-end training approach could benefit NCM by aligning
representation learning with the generative objective. Our main experiments use generators of
roughly 100M parameters, and our scaling study extends to 725M parameters on LM1B. Scaling to
larger datasets and model sizes is another exciting direction. Generations are generally fluent but
exhibit occasional artifacts at coarse-level transitions, presenting another opportunity to further
improve NCM.
