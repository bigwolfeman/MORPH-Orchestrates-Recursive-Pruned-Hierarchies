A BSTRACT
Looped (weight-tied) transformers enable adaptive computation by allocating
more iterations to harder inputs. Fixed-Point Reasoners (FPRM) achieve this with
a damped fixed-point solver, but operate in an unbounded Euclidean residual space
with coupled rescaling and relative-norm halting. We introduce Spherical FixedPoint Reasoners (SFPRM), which performs the same computation on the unit
hypersphere of normalized transformers (nGPT). Input injection, damping, and
halting become geodesic operations, yielding boundedness by construction and
eliminating coefficient coupling. We prove fixed-point existence, local convergence under an explicit step-size condition, and global convergence under strong
injection. On Sudoku-Extreme, Maze-Hard and Mini-ARC, SFPRM achieves
96.6%, 87.9% and 59.1% test accuracy, respectively, outperforming FPRM and
other baselines while allocating test-time compute more effectively. Its converged
geometry is also interpretable: token–input angles capture puzzle structure and
predict correctness.

I NTRODUCTION

Scaling test-time computation has become the main lever for improving the reasoning ability of sequence models, most visibly through chain-of-thought traces (Wei et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023;
Cobbe et al., 2021; Yao et al., 2023; Snell et al., 2025). Looped transformers offer a latent alternative (Dehghani et al., 2019; Bai et al., 2019; Geiping et al., 2025): a single weight-tied block is
applied repeatedly, so depth becomes an iteration count chosen at inference time, and compute can
be allocated per input according to its difficulty. On algorithmic puzzles such as Sudoku, mazes, and
ARC, this recipe lets small recursive reasoners compete with far larger models (Wang et al., 2025;
Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025; Movahedi et al., 2026).
A looped transformer raises two questions: when should it stop, and will it stay well-behaved as
it runs? The fixed-point view (Bai et al., 2019), answers the first by treating the loop as a solver
for z⋆ = Fθ (z⋆ ; x) and halting once the iterate stops moving. Fixed-Point Reasoners (FPRM)
(Movahedi et al., 2026) turn this into a practical recipe: a damped iteration whose step size decays
on a patience rule, a relative-norm residual as the halting test, and a boundedness theorem that keeps
the recurrent state from diverging. The second question is where the Euclidean formulation shows its
cost. Because the residual stream is free to grow, a boundedness theorem must couple four residualrescaling coefficients, and the halting residual needs a normalization factor to be scale-invariant.
We introduce Spherical Fixed-Point Reasoning Models (SFPRM), which run FPRM’s damped solver
on nGPT’s (Loshchilov et al., 2025) unit sphere (Figure 1). The normalized Transformer (nGPT)
obtains stable dynamics by changing the geometry rather than adding control: every hidden state
lives on the unit hypersphere Sd−1 and each sub-layer is a learned angular move followed by renormalization, so the divergence FPRM must guard against cannot occur. Every control quantity of
the loop becomes an angle: the input is re-injected by moving a fraction λ of the great-circle arc
toward it, the damped update is a spherical linear interpolation (Slerp) whose coefficient ηi is an
angular step size that FPRM’s patience-based decay literally shrinks, and the halting residual is the
worst-token angle between an iterate and its proposal. Our contributions are:

R ELATED W ORK

Looped and weight-tied transformers. Tying the weights of a transformer block (Vaswani et al.,
2017) and applying it repeatedly turns depth into an iteration count at inference time (Dehghani
et al., 2019; Lan et al., 2020). Looped models match much deeper non-looped ones on synthetic reasoning tasks and can simulate chain-of-thought in latent space (Saunshi et al., 2025), learn in-context
algorithms more sample-efficiently (Yang et al., 2024; Gatmiry et al., 2024), emulate programmable
computation (Giannou et al., 2023), generalize in length when the loop matches the algorithm’s recursion (Fan et al., 2025), scale to billions of parameters (Geiping et al., 2025; Prairie et al., 2026),
and supply a scaling axis for masked diffusion models (Carballo-Castro et al., 2026; Miele et al.,
2026). SFPRM is a looped transformer whose loop is run as a fixed-point solve on the sphere.
Equilibrium models, adaptive computation, and halting. Adaptive Computation Time (Graves,
2016) and PonderNet (Banino et al., 2021) learn a halting distribution through an auxiliary loss,
so halting is a learned head rather than a property of the dynamics. Recent methods such as HRM
(Wang et al., 2025), URM (Gao et al., 2025), TRM (Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025), and PTRM (Sghaier
et al., 2026) inherit this design. Deep equilibrium models (Bai et al., 2019; 2020; Winston & Kolter,
2020; Bai et al., 2021) replace the unrolled iteration by the fixed point of a weight-tied layer and
differentiate implicitly through it. Attractor models (Fein-Ashley & Rashidinejad, 2026) and equilibrium reasoners (Huang et al., 2026) make the loop’s attractor explicit and halt on a solver tolerance or a residual-based selection, and FPRM (Movahedi et al., 2026) halts on a scale-invariant
residual of the tied block itself, at the cost of coupled residual-rescaling coefficients that keep its
Euclidean residual stream bounded; iterating past the point where the answer has settled is itself

Normalized and hyperspherical representations. Constraining representations to a sphere is a
long-standing stabilization device, for parameters (Salimans & Kingma, 2016; Miyato et al., 2018),

3.2.1

T HEORETICAL GUARANTEES

We now investigate what the geometry buys analytically. All statements concern the exact recurrence
eqs. (8) to (10) with shortest-arc interpolation. Full proofs are in Appendix A.
Boundedness by construction. Because injection and damping return unit token vectors
(Lemma 1), the block does so by construction (eq. (5)), and so does every branch of the guarded step
(Lemma 2), the state stays on S for every i and every θ: recurrent magnitudes can neither explode
nor vanish, which is what FPRM’s boundedness theorem secures through the coefficient constraints
in eq. (2). However, boundedness alone says nothing about whether a fixed point exists or is reached,
which is what we address next.
Input injection guarantees a fixed point. On a sphere, continuity alone does not guarantee
a fixed point since rotating a circle moves every point. Input injection repairs this by moving each token a fraction λ of the way to its input, keeping every injected state in the cap
Cx := {y ∈ S : ∠(yt , xt ) ≤ (1 − λ)π for every token t}, which, unlike the sphere, can be flattened
into a closed ball, where every continuous self-map has a fixed point.
Proposition 1 (Fixed-point existence). Fix x and 0 < λ < 1. Assume fθ is continuous on Cx and
fθ (y)t ̸= −xt for every y ∈ Cx and token t. Then the recurrent map Fθ (· ; x) in eq. (8) has at least
one fixed point z⋆ = Fθ (z⋆ ; x) in S.
The full proof (Appendix A.4) applies Brouwer’s theorem (Brouwer, 1911; Ortega & Rheinboldt,
1970) to the reversed composition H(y) := Slerpλ (fθ (y), x). Unlike Fθ , which injects before the
block, H ends with the injection, so it maps the cap into itself, and the antipode assumption keeps
it well defined and continuous; its fixed point y⋆ gives the fixed point z⋆ := fθ (y⋆ ) of Fθ . No
Lipschitz constant, contraction modulus, or spectral bound on fθ is needed. FPRM proves existence
only via global convergence, hence under contraction (Movahedi et al., 2026, Theorem 2); Brouwer
alone would need the coefficient coupling that keeps a ball invariant. On the sphere compactness is
structural and injection supplies the missing ball-shaped domain. Only y⋆ , however, provably lies
in Cx , and existence implies neither uniqueness nor attraction, which the next two results address.
Angular damping stabilizes a fixed point. Let z⋆ be a fixed point of Fθ (· ; x) and let J :=
Dz Fθ (z⋆ ; x) be its Jacobian in the recurrent state at z⋆ , with θ and x held fixed. Since Fθ maps
S to itself, J sends tangent perturbations u = (ut )Tt=1 with ⟨ut , zt⋆ ⟩ = 0 to tangent perturbations,
and we regard it as an endomorphism of that T (d − 1)-dimensional tangent space. It contains all
coupling between tokens through attention and need be neither orthogonal nor diagonalizable.

Global convergence under strong injection. Finally, we identify a regime where the raw recurrence (η = 1) converges from every admissible initialization. x and fθ are held fixed while λ varies.
Theorem 2 (Global convergence under strong input injection). Suppose fθ : S → S is continuously
differentiable in a neighborhood of x and fθ (x)t ̸= −xt for every token t. Then there exists a
λ0 ∈ (0, 1), depending on fθ and x, such that for every λ ∈ (λ0 , 1) the map Fθ (· ; x) has a fixed
point z⋆ , unique among all states at which the input interpolation is defined. For each such λ and
every z0 ∈ S with z0,t ̸= −xt for all t, the iterates zi+1 = Fθ (zi ; x) are well defined and satisfy
∥zi − z⋆ ∥ ≤ Cq i−1 for all i ≥ 1, with C > 0 and q ∈ (0, 1) independent of z0 .
The proof (Appendix A.7, with an explicit λ0 ) uses logarithmic coordinates at x, where injection
multiplies by 1 − λ: for λ near one, the first injection pulls every admissible state into a small ball
around x on which the map is a Banach contraction, so only that ball matters. FPRM’s global result
(Movahedi et al., 2026, Theorem 2) instead rests on a global Lipschitz bound and a small residual
coefficient, which the sphere lacks: injection does not contract near the input’s antipode. Together
the three results cover every injection strength: a fixed point exists for all λ, strong injection makes
it a unique global attractor, and damping gives local convergence under weaker injection.

RQ2: CONVERGENCE AND HALTING

Loop utilization and halting. To see how the two solvers converge and when they stop, we run
each trained model with halting disabled for Nmax iterations, decode every iterate zi , and record the
residual its solver tests. Halting is counted per sequence, i.e., a sequence halts once its residual stays
below the threshold (Appendix C.4). Figure 3 shows the result on Maze and Figure 10 on Sudoku.
Both residuals fall quickly, and both models saturate their accuracy within 20–35 iterations, so every
later loop is spent after the answer stops changing. SFPRM converges faster and stops sooner: it
halts on 992/1000 mazes against FPRM’s 950, and on average 22 iterations earlier (108 vs. 130).
Fixed-point uniqueness. Theorem 2 predicts that for λ > λ0 the raw recurrence zi+1 = Fθ (zi ; x)
contracts to one fixed point from every initialization. We test this by overriding λ at inference in the
trained model (learned λ = 0.47) and running the raw map, without damping, in fp32 for 400 steps
on 32 evenly spaced test puzzles, from eight initializations each: six random states, the input x,
and its antipode −x, which the theorem excludes. The largest geodesic angle between the eight end
states measures uniqueness without a reference point (Figure 4a). For λ ≥ 0.8 all eight end states
coincide on every puzzle. At the trained λ = 0.47 the raw map reaches an attracting fixed point on
91% of the puzzles, and on the puzzle of Figure 4b all eight paths end inside the exact-match region.
At λ = 0.1 the raw iteration does not converge, while for λ ≥ 0.7 it converges within a few steps to
a unique fixed point that no longer solves the puzzle.

We introduced SFPRM, which runs FPRM’s
damped fixed-point loop on nGPT’s hypersphere.
This change of geometry assures norm boundedness by construction and eliminates the need for
FPRM’s coupled residual-rescaling coefficients.
We provide theoretical guarantees under weaker
assumptions than its Euclidean counterpart. Empirically, on puzzle and reasoning tasks, SFPRM
halts more effectively at equal or higher accuracy than FPRM. Finally, we showed that the
angle each token keeps to its input turns out to
be a readable encoding of the task’s structure
and, on Sudoku, a label-free predictor of correctness. Natural next steps are to scale SFPRM to
language modeling and train with better solvers,
which we have so far used only at inference.

B.2

M ODELS

SFPRM is implemented in the FPRM code base and shares an identical backbone with 6.83M trainable parameters: hidden size 512, 8 attention heads, MLP expansion 4, rotary position embeddings,
and the configuration fields H_cycles/L_cycles/L_layers = 3/6/2 of the code base, run as
a single weight-tied loop without hierarchy. The number of iterations backpropagated through, is
the BPTT window K = 6 of Section 3.1.
FPRM. FPRM uses a pre-norm block with the coupled residual-rescaling coefficients
(α1 , β1 , α2 , β2 ) of Section 3.1, linear damping, and the relative residual rE with tolerance τE = 0.1.
SFPRM. The spherical model replaces the block by the nGPT-style block of Section 3.2: QKnormalized attention and a SwiGLU MLP, each folded into the unit-norm state by an eigen-learningrate retraction, with all weight vectors renormalized after every optimizer step. Three further choices
are specific to SFPRM. (i) The input is injected by the geodesic step of eq. (8) with a learned fraction
λ, initialized at 0.1. (ii) The readout is unnormalized: the rows of the output head are not constrained
to unit norm, since with a unit-norm state that constraint would cap every logit at ±sz , and with it
the confidence of the predictions. (iii) The solver is the geodesic one of Algorithm 1, with the
chordal residual of eq. (9) and tolerance τc = 0.01, which corresponds to an angular tolerance of
τang = arccos(0.99) ≈ 8.1◦ per token.
B.3

T RAINING AND SOLVER SETTINGS

Table 3 lists every hyperparameter. Unless stated otherwise, SFPRM trains with a constant learning
rate after the warmup steps and an exponential moving average of the weights with rate 0.999. We
optimize SFPRM’s 2-D weight matrices with Muon (Jordan et al., 2024; Pethick et al., 2025) at
learning rate 5 × 10−3 and momentum 0.95, and its embeddings, normalization scales, and scalar
parameters with AdamW at 10−3 . Weight decay is 0.1 for SFPRM, and the per-puzzle embeddings
have their own learning rate. SFPRM additionally uses weight dropout and variational dropout
of 0.1 on the main build (variational dropout only on the 1k×1000 Sudoku run). Following the
HRM/FPRM code base, one epoch draws one augmented example for every base puzzle; hence
12,000 epochs on the 5k×200 build and 60,000 epochs on the 1k×1000 build and on Maze-Hard
all amount to the same 78,125 optimizer steps at a global batch of 768. For the final Sudoku results,
we train for 15,000 epochs.
Deep supervision. Training runs one segment of K = 6 solver iterations per optimizer step and
backpropagates through all K geodesic steps of the segment; the recurrent state is carried across
optimizer steps and detached between segments, and a sample is re-initialized once it halts or once
it has been unrolled for max_iter = 12 iterations. The halting residual, patience counter, step-size
schedule, and iterate bookkeeping are evaluated on detached states, so they steer the solver without
entering the gradient.
Solver. Both geodesic steps of SFPRM use the guarded interpolation gSlerp of eq. (12). Per sample,
the solver keeps the current step size η, the patience counter, the residual, and the residual state. As
in FPRM, η starts at 1 and is multiplied by the decay factor γ whenever the residual has not improved
for P = 5 consecutive iterations. The loop halts when the residual drops below tolerance, when η
falls below the floor ηmin , or when the iteration budget is exhausted.

E IGEN LR AND OTHER LEARNABLE PARAMETERS DURING TRAINING

Figure 12 tracks the learned scalars of SFPRM during training on Maze and Sudoku. Both models
raise the injection fraction from 0.1 to λ = 0.575 on Maze and λ = 0.470 on Sudoku, with most
of the increase in the first 30k of 78k training steps. This moves them toward the strong-injection
regime of Theorem 2. On Sudoku, all eight initializations already reach the same fixed point on
72% of puzzles at the learned λ (Figure 4). The logit scale sz grows almost linearly from 16 to
76, at nearly the same rate on both tasks, and is still rising when training ends. The eigen learning
rates keep means near their initial 0.5, similarly as noted by Loshchilov et al. (2025). However,
individual dimensions spread widely, from negative values, which move the state away from the
sub-layer output, to values up to 2.6.

Neither Anderson’s nor Broyden’s method computes a Jacobian or any gradient. Both are quasiNewton methods that infer curvature from secants, the observed changes of state and residual between steps, so each step still costs one evaluation of Fθ . With an empty memory both take exactly
the fixed step. Both act on the whole state of an example at once, all tokens flattened into one vector,
with one history per example in a batch. Their extrapolated points leave the sphere and are projected
back by Π. The extra work per step is a few vector operations on the state and an m × m linear
solve, and the extra memory is m stored copies of the state, both small next to an evaluation of Fθ .
As safeguards, a non-finite extrapolation falls back to the fixed step, and Broyden skips an update
whose denominator is near zero. Both methods use the step size η = 1 (no damping).

The undamped step does not halt reliably. The fixed step never reaches τ on 37%, 100%, 49%
and 56% of the examples in the four settings. For SFPRM on Maze its answers are identical to the
default solver’s, but on Sudoku it is less accurate for both models. For SFPRM on Sudoku a quarter
of the puzzles do not halt within 1000 steps.
Anderson acceleration matches the default solver at a fraction of the cost. Its accuracy at the
halt is within one point of the default solver’s in all four settings. Anderson halts earlier in every
setting, with a striking 37 against 435 for SFPRM on Sudoku. Averaged over all examples, this is 9×
fewer evaluations of Fθ . For FPRM on Sudoku the saving disappears (190 against 200 evaluations):
10.8% of the puzzles never reach τ under Anderson, and all of them are answered incorrectly.
Broyden’s method reaches the tolerance threshold quickly but does not stay there. On Maze it
halts as early as Anderson (14 and 16 median steps), and its halted answers are as accurate as the
default solver’s. By continuing to iterate beyond the tolerance, however, it leaves the fixed point and
its accuracy degrades, making it usable only with halting.
On Sudoku, halting certifies the answer. For SFPRM on Sudoku, 99.7–100% of the puzzles a
solver halts on are solved exactly, so the accuracy differences come entirely from the puzzles it fails
to halt on. The same holds less strictly for FPRM (96.2% of the default solver’s halted puzzles
are solved, 99.6% of Anderson’s). On Maze, halting does not certify the answer: incorrect mazes
converge too, and for the solvers that halt on almost every example the accuracy among halted
examples is about the same as the overall accuracy.
Trajectories in the principal plane. Figure 18 shows the four solvers at the trained λ in the plane
construction of Figure 4b, on a single test example from each task, from eight initializations. The
reference z⋆ is Anderson’s end point, a fixed point to fp32 precision that decodes both examples
exactly. On Maze, Anderson halts at iteration 13 from all eight initializations, the default solver
crosses the tolerance at a median of 140 iterations but ends 0.06 rad away, and fixed η = 1 never
crosses it. On the Sudoku puzzle only Anderson halts (median 40), while the default and fixed
solvers end 0.16 and 0.33 rad from z⋆ and still decode the puzzle exactly. Broyden reaches the
tolerance as early as Anderson and then leaves from most starts.
Trajectories in polar coordinates. Figures 19 and 20 follow one token per cell class under each
solver in the polar view of Figure 5b, for the maze of that panel and for a Sudoku puzzle. The radius
is the geodesic angle to a common reference, Anderson’s end point after 2000 iterations, a fixed
point to fp32 precision that decodes both examples exactly. Inward is convergence toward z⋆ , a held
radius is orbiting, and outward is leaving. Anderson ends within 10−4 degrees of the reference. The
default solver ends 0.005–0.14◦ away on the mazes and up to 2.1◦ on the Sudoku puzzles, after

and Broyden reaches the tolerance first and then leaves to 60–103◦ .

D

L IMITATIONS

On the theory side, Theorem 1 certifies a step-size window but not that the patience rule finds it, and
Theorem 2 covers only strong injection. On the experimental side, every run uses a single seed and
neither model was tuned beyond FPRM’s recipe: a broader hyperparameter search, stronger regularization, and optimizers that exploit the spherical geometry further would likely improve both. Two
directions remain open. Attention-free MLP-Mixer blocks have been reported to beat Transformer
blocks on some of these puzzles (Jolicoeur-Martineau, 2025; Sghaier et al., 2026; Suleymanzade
et al., 2026); the spherical loop is agnostic to the block and could be combined with them. And
we have not applied SFPRM to language modeling, where looped Transformers are gaining ground
(Geiping et al., 2025; Prairie et al., 2026) and where halting on a fixed angular tolerance may be
most valuable.
